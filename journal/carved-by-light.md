Consider the peacock above. It has no colour. Every feather of its fanned tail is the same grey plaster as the wall it stands on, and yet the composition reads clearly from across the room, modelled entirely by one wash of light falling from the cove above it. Cover that light and the wall goes mute. Move it, and the same carving becomes a different artwork.

This is the second material every relief is made of. The first is timber, plaster, bronze, or stone. The other is light, and unlike the first, it changes by the hour.

## The Jali Lesson

South Asian architecture has been designing with projected light for centuries. The pierced screen, the *jali*, is the tradition's clearest statement of the principle: a wall that does its finest work not on its own surface but on the floors and walls around it, casting geometric shadows that stretch, sharpen, and dissolve as the sun crosses the sky. Our own craft notes describe pierced bronze and brass screens as instruments that filter daylight into "evolving geometric shadows," and the phrase repays attention. Evolving. A jali is not an image; it is a schedule of images.

The curatorial lesson generalises to every carved surface we translate. A relief commissioned without a lighting intention is half a commission.

## Raking Light and the Grammar of Depth

Light that grazes a surface at a shallow angle, raking light, is the great amplifier of texture. Under frontal illumination a two-millimetre carving stroke nearly vanishes; under a grazing wash it throws a shadow several times its own depth. This is why the micro-line matrices of Gond work, the herringbone dashes and stippled dots, are specified in our studies with raking light in mind: the texture is calibrated to catch it.

The practical grammar is short.

- **Angle:** The shallower the light, the louder the relief. Cove and grazing positions dramatise; frontal track positions flatten and clarify.
- **Beam and rendering:** Focused gallery lighting, narrow beams with high colour rendering, is specified in our framed studies at CRI 95 and above, so that pigment and ground keep their intended relationship after dark.
- **Consistency:** A relief lit from two competing directions develops crossed shadows and loses its drawing. One dominant direction, deliberately chosen, is worth more than lumens.

> Pigment tells a wall what to say. Light decides whether anyone hears it.

## Night Architecture

Some of our studies invert the arrangement and put the light behind the work. The darkened bronze screen glows amber through every pierced line after dusk; the etched glass partition carries illumination through its own body, fed from the edges; the backlit resin panel behind a vitality pool reads as a lantern rather than a window. In each case the artwork changes register between day and night, sculptural in the morning, luminous in the evening, and the specification carries the transition explicitly, with dim-to-warm systems that descend toward candlelight temperatures as the hour grows late.

This is worth stating plainly because architectural lighting is so often an afterthought applied to finished rooms. In these assemblies it is a component of the artwork, named in the same specification study as the metal gauge and the fire rating.

## A Note in Praise of Shadow

Junichirō Tanizaki's celebrated essay on Japanese aesthetics argued that traditional interiors were designed around darkness, that lacquer and gold were meant to be discovered by candlelight rather than exposed by the modern bulb. South Asian sacred architecture holds a parallel intuition: the innermost sanctum of a temple is its darkest chamber, and ornament grows quieter as one approaches it. We resist the urge to flatten these traditions into one claim. But both counsel the same restraint to a contemporary specifier: do not light everything. A carved wall needs its shadows the way a voice needs its pauses, and the most common failure we see in renderings is not too little light but too much, everywhere, always.

The finest compliment a visitor can pay one of these walls is to return at a different hour and find it changed.
