We remember rooms by how they sound almost as vividly as by how they look. The library hush that slows the voice without a posted sign. The restaurant where conversation curdles into din by eight o'clock. The lobby whose marble splendour somehow exhausts its visitors before the elevator arrives. Sound is the atmosphere we breathe with our ears, and in contemporary interiors it has become the scarcest luxury of all.

This essay makes a simple curatorial argument: the acoustic surface, usually the least loved object in a specification, is one of the most natural homes for South Asian heritage art. The disciplines meet more gracefully than either side expects.

## The Ear Enters the Room First

Modern architecture is built from acoustically unforgiving materials. Glass, honed stone, polished concrete, and gypsum board are all excellent reflectors, and a room composed entirely of reflectors keeps every sound in circulation long after its usefulness has ended. Acousticians describe this lingering as reverberation, and a century of concert-hall research, from Wallace Sabine's early measurements onward, has given the profession precise tools for taming it: absorption to remove sound energy, and diffusion to scatter what remains.

What the research literature also suggests, and what anyone who has worked in an open-plan office already knows, is that uncontrolled sound is not a neutral inconvenience. It taxes attention. A room that flatters the eye while assaulting the ear is not a finished room.

## Relief Is Diffusion, Fiber Is Absorption

Here is the quiet coincidence at the center of this essay. The two physical strategies of room acoustics correspond, almost line for line, to two ancient instincts of South Asian craft.

- **Relief is diffusion.** A carved surface scatters sound the way it scatters light. The fluted timber, layered plaster, and stepped bas-relief that this atelier's lineages naturally produce break up flat reflections into softer, more even energy. Our own translatability studies noted that Gond's micro-line matrices and carved hardwood panels lend "subtle acoustic diffusion" to expansive walls; the observation began as an aside and grew into a practice.
- **Fiber is absorption.** Wool felt, layered textile, and dense natural fiber do what stone cannot: they let sound in and decline to give it back. The *Kūrma* composition pictured above is built in exactly this register, copper-detailed motifs set into deep charcoal acoustic felt, so that the artwork and the absorber are one object rather than neighbours.

![The Kūrma motif up close: copper detail set into deep charcoal acoustic felt, the artwork and the absorber as one object.](assets/img/journal/kurma-acoustic-panel.jpg)

Performance in this territory is measured, not promised. Absorption is rated by the Noise Reduction Coefficient under ASTM C423, and the honest caveat belongs in every conversation: NRC values are mounting-dependent, and a panel's rating travels with its installation method, not with its photograph. We keep that caveat printed in our specification studies because a wellness suite deserves arithmetic, not adjectives.

> A carved wall works on the ear twice: once by what it absorbs, and once by what it scatters. The ornament was never only ornament.

## What the Lineages Already Knew

It is tempting to claim that the mandapa halls of temple architecture, with their densely carved pillars and coffered ceilings, were deliberate acoustic instruments. We hold that claim lightly; the builders left us geometry, not measurement data. What can be said with confidence is narrower and more interesting. Craft traditions that never flattened their surfaces, that layered textile against stone, that repeated motifs at several scales across a wall, produced rooms whose sonic character differed audibly from the hard-shelled interiors we build today. The heritage surface arrives already articulated, and articulation is precisely what a reflective room lacks.

## Specifying Quiet

For the architect or interior designer, the practical questions are familiar, and heritage art does not exempt a wall from any of them.

- **Rating:** Request the NRC value with its test mounting, and treat the pairing as inseparable.
- **Placement:** Absorptive and diffusive surfaces earn their keep where reflections concentrate, along the long parallel walls of lounges and the rear walls of rooms where speech matters.
- **Depth:** Diffusion is a function of relief depth relative to wavelength; shallow texture flatters light, deeper carving works harder on sound. The two ambitions can be calibrated together.
- **Fire and hygiene:** The compliance questions, flame spread under ASTM E84 and cleanability in wellness contexts, are answered in each of our specification studies, because an artwork that cannot pass plan review is a rendering, not a proposal.

A room can be beautiful and merciless. It can also be beautiful and kind. The difference is rarely visible in a photograph, which is exactly why it belongs in the specification.
